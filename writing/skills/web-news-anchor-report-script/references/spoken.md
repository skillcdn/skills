# The voice: sentences said, not read

How each sentence of an anchor report is worded so that it sounds like an anchor telling a viewer the news and not like an article read aloud: its length, its words, the form that says how it is known, how one sentence hands on to the next, and what the ear needs. Read before the first sentence of phase 3 and again at check 2. [script.md](script.md) holds what the report tells and in what order.

The sections hold for any language. The last one is the record, by language, of what users corrected in real scripts: the place a new correction goes.

## Said, not read

A script is speech written down, and its test is the mouth. Say each sentence at a newsreader's pace to someone in the room who has not read the brief. What nobody would say that way is the article showing through: a clause folded inside a clause, a word out of a document, a condition recited for completeness, the same ending sentence after sentence. The sections below are that test taken apart, and none of them is a fact check: a sentence can be true, sourced and clear, and still be written.

## One breath, one point

- **The point, in the words it needs.** A written sentence carries its conditions with it. A spoken one carries the point, and a qualifier stays only where the sentence would be false, or heard as something else, without it: "parking in a marked lane is fined", not "a vehicle left in a marked lane without the permit issued beforehand is fined". The exact terms of a rule are the article's, and the screen can hold them.
- **Two points are two sentences.** A sentence that gives a setting and then a point is said as two, the first handing on to the second ("Sentences hand on", below). A reason given for a statement is a sentence of its own, after it.
- **One breath.** A sentence the anchor cannot say in a breath is two. Measure it when the length is counted, as the spoken count over the number of sentences: for Korean, about thirty syllables a sentence across the report, and a sentence past fifty is split (illustrative).
- **A subject sits close to its verb.** A long clause before the noun it describes is heard as words with no owner: split it in two, the noun in the first sentence and what is said of it in the next.

## The words of the newscast

- **The common word.** For what a body is doing (looking into it, reviewing it, deciding by a date) the viewer knows a few words from every night's news, and those are the ones to use; in Korean, 조사 중입니다 or 검토하고 있습니다. A rarer word chosen for precision or for variety makes the viewer hear the word and miss the sentence. [sentences.md](/docs/writing/sentences.md) holds plain words for any text.
- **The thing, not a stand-in.** A sentence that says what something is ends on the noun that names it (a measure, a plan, the reason), and not on a filler that only closes the clause; in Korean, -대책입니다 or -이유입니다 where the draft has -ㄴ 것입니다. The noun tells the viewer what kind of thing they just heard, and the sentence is shorter by it.
- **A name comes with what it is** at its first mention ("the agency that issues the permits"), and by its short form after that. A short form the viewer was never given is a new unknown.

## How it is known

An anchor reports. The form of each sentence says how the newsroom knows what it says. A plain statement of what someone else does, demands or expects is heard as the newsroom vouching for it. [facts.md](/docs/writing/facts.md) "A hedge" gives the rule for any text; in a spoken report it sits in the ending or the attribution of every sentence. This is attribution and not softening: a count is stated flat, as its counter's count, and the "reportedly" with no one behind it that facts.md rules out stays out.

| The sentence carries | Its form | In Korean, for example |
|---|---|---|
| Something going on now | The progressive, or what is seen | -고 있습니다, -는 모습입니다 |
| A side's demand, position or account | That side as the subject, ongoing or as its position | -라는 입장입니다, -라고 설명하고 있습니다, -라고 주장하고 있습니다 |
| A count or an outcome | With who counted, and up to when | -로 집계됐습니다, -ㄴ 것으로 나타났습니다 |
| What a person said | With who and in what capacity; what another outlet reported or analysed, as heard through it, the outlet named on screen | -라고 말했습니다, -ㄴ 것으로 전해졌습니다, -라는 분석이 나왔습니다 |
| A plan or an announcement | As announced, with its condition | -ㄹ 예정입니다, -기로 했습니다 |
| What may follow | As an outlook, naming what decides it | -ㄹ 전망입니다, -ㄹ 수 있습니다 |
| A definition, or a fact of the record | Stated | -입니다 |

A language without such endings carries the same in attribution and tense ("has said", "is expected to", "by the ministry's count").

## Sentences hand on

Speech moves by setting up and landing: one sentence gives the viewer the setting, and the next gives the point. Where the language has an ending that leaves a sentence open toward the next, the setting takes it and the point takes the plain one. In Korean the open endings are -는데요, -했는데요 and -인데요, and the plain ones -습니다 and -입니다.

- The open ending is a promise that the next sentence is what this one was for. It goes on a setting, a turn or the first half of a contrast, and the sentence after it lands.
- It is mixed in, not alternated: about one sentence in four, never two running, and never the report's last.
- It joins the form that says how the content is known and does not replace it: -라고 밝혔는데요, -로 집계됐는데요, -ㄹ 예정인데요.
- It is the join a written comma made. "A, and so B" in one sentence becomes the setting and the point in two, each short enough to say.

A language without such an ending does the same with length and order: a short sentence that sets up, then the one that lands. Every sentence on one ending, whichever it is, is a signal of machine-written text.

## For the ear

- **One number to a sentence**, with the thing it counts. Two leave the viewer unsure which was which and whether one is part of the other. A change from one value to another is one number, said as the pair. Say a figure rounded, as people say it, and put the exact figure on the screen at the same moment.
- **Two of a kind.** Of parallel things (causes, demands, measures) the ear keeps two. Tell the two the sources weigh most, a sentence or two each, and say first that there are two where the sources group them so. A third is passed in a clause or left out. More hold only as a chain the sources state, each step the cause of the next: a chain is one line, and a list is several.
- **Dates are said as the viewer lives them**, counted from the air date: yesterday, next Monday, the day before the vote. The absolute date follows the word in parentheses and is not read, so that the script can be dated again for another day. This is where the script leaves the writing set, which writes dates absolute.
- **A quote is spoken as a quote only when it is short and stands alone.** A longer one is said in plain words with its speaker, because the ear loses where a long quote ends, and the words as printed go on the screen as the quote. Words cut from the middle of a sentence keep what they point at, or are said plainly: half a quote that refers to something unsaid is a new question.
- **A person another outlet interviewed is that outlet's reporting.** The screen names the outlet with the quote.

## Korean

What users corrected in Korean scripts, each under the habit it shows, as the phrase before and after and never a run's sentence, name or figure. A new correction goes into the list it belongs to, and one that shows a new habit adds its rule above first. A run lists the corrections its user made in its delivery, each as the phrase before and after, for whoever maintains the skill. Add a language section when a run teaches one.

- **A condition out of the rule's text**, cut to the point: "미리 신고하지 않고 세워 두기만 해도" as "세워 두기만 해도". The sentence was true and clear, and was heard as long.
- **A rarer verb for what a body is doing**, replaced by the newscast's: "-는지 가리고 있습니다" as "-는지 조사 중입니다".
- **것 as the predicate**, replaced by the noun: "-가 내놓은 것입니다" as "-가 내놓은 대책입니다". The news forms in -ㄴ 것으로 (-ㄴ 것으로 나타났습니다, -ㄴ 것으로 전해졌습니다) are not this, and stay.
- **Every sentence on -습니다**, mixed with the open endings: "A했는데, B였습니다" in one sentence as "A했는데요. B였습니다." in two, and a count said as "-는 …건인데요." before the cases that show it.
- **A long clause before its noun**, split in two with the noun first: "…하고 …해서 …한 조치입니다" as "…한 조치입니다. 이 조치로 …했습니다."
- **Causes told as a list of four**, cut to two and announced, with whose view it is: "…에서는 원인을 두 가지로 꼽습니다. 하나는 …, 다른 하나는 …"
- **A close heard as an end**: an outlook that names what decides it, "-에서 판가름 날 전망입니다", and one outlet's outlook said as one, "-ㄹ 수 있다는 전망이 나옵니다".
- **The length of a sentence**: scripts that averaged well over thirty syllables a sentence, each one clear to its listeners, were read as long by the user who reviewed them.
