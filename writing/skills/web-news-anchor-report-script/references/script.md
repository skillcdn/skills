# The script: words a viewer hears once

How the script of an anchor report is written so that a viewer follows it in one hearing. The pack's writing set ([readers.md](/docs/writing/readers.md), [sentences.md](/docs/writing/sentences.md), [facts.md](/docs/writing/facts.md)) holds what every text shares. This page is its sharper form for a spoken news script and wins where they differ: the dates, the form that says how a thing is known, and the close.

## One hearing

A reader can stop and look back. A viewer cannot: a sentence not understood as it passes is lost, and the next is heard through the question it left. Before a sentence is kept, ask what the viewer knows at that second and what they are asking. The sentence answers that question, or it is in the wrong place.

Each sentence also starts from what the viewer already holds: what the last one ended on, or a word that says the turn (one, the other, on top of this, meanwhile). One that opens on a new name with no such tie is heard as the start of another story.

## The thread

Write the report in one sentence before any of it: what happened, what it does to the viewer, and what decides what comes next. The thread is the brief's angle said plainly, never a reading of the writer's own; where the brief says the story has no angle yet, it is what happened and what is pending. Everything in the script serves it.

- A fact enters with its link to the thread, said in the script in a clause the brief supports. A fact that is true, sourced and interesting, and whose link the script cannot say, is left out: the viewer hears it as the start of a second story and waits for it to matter.
- A report of this length carries one story. A neighbouring event (another body's action, a date that falls nearby) belongs only where a source says how it bears on this one, and the script says it too.
- The link may be as plain as time. The story's own latest step, what the same body, place or dispute did after the main event, is told as that, in its place before the close. What a source must state is a cause, or how one thing bears on another, never that the next step happened.

## The lead

The lead is the viewer's reason to keep watching.

- Start where the viewer stands: what the story does to people like them, and in the same sentence who acted, as its cause. A lead that opens on the body that acted gives the viewer an institution's news; one that opens on the effect gives them their own.
- Choose the opening picture with the opening sentence: the one scene that shows that effect. The sentence does not describe the picture, and the picture shows what the sentence says.
- The lead is as strong as the facts. Where the sources say an effect is feared, the lead says it is feared; where they count it, the lead states the count.
- One thing, in one or two sentences, then the handover: one short line that sends the viewer to the screen, in the words the language's newscasts use, carrying no fact. The lead is the shortest part of the report.
- The largest thing still to come (a decision, a date, a step someone has announced) is told once, with its condition, where the story reaches it, which is usually the end. Told in the lead without its condition and later with it, the viewer hears two facts that disagree. The title on screen may name it from the start.

## Parts and their jobs

After the handover the anchor's voice runs over the screen. The parts come in the order the viewer's questions do.

| Part | The viewer is asking | It holds |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | What is this thing I just heard named? | The term or the measure, defined, and what it does to the viewer |
| What it looks like | What does that mean where I stand? | One scene or one witness |
| What happened | So how did it go? | The point first, then the count or the case that shows it |
| Why | Why is this happening? | The causes the sources give, or what each side wants, in its own terms and at its own strength, the reply beside the claim |
| What comes next | Where does this go? | The story's latest step, then the close |

A story need not have every part, and a part is one to four sentences: more, and it is answering a second question. The order changes where the story's own logic asks for it, never to put the actors before the viewer.

## A sentence has one job

Each sentence does one of these: it defines, shows, states a result, gives a cause or a side's position, says what comes next, or hands over. Name the job in a few words before the sentence is kept. A sentence whose job cannot be named holds two jobs or none, and a listener says of it that they cannot tell what it is for.

- A definition is written as one: the term, what it is, and what it does to the viewer, in that order. A list of what people are doing under the term is a scene, and leaves the viewer to work the meaning out; where the report needs both, they are two sentences, the definition first.
- The point comes before the detail that carries it. A detail given alone ("the shelves were half empty by noon") hands the viewer an observation to interpret. Say what it showed, then the detail.
- The first sentence after the handover answers the question the lead raised, which is usually what the named thing is.

## Links the sources state

A cause, a consequence and how one event bears on another are facts like any other, and come from the brief.

- Two things joined in one sentence have a relation the source states. "As A happened, B" and "A, so B" are claims about cause, whatever the connective.
- A case is told only when its sources say why one thing led to the next. Where they give the events and not the cause, no wording repairs it: a listener asks "why did that follow?" of every version. The case goes back to the brief's articles, and without an answer it is left out, while the count it belonged to stays.
- A cause from elsewhere (another place, another body, the day before) is told through to the step that reaches the story's own subject, each step a source's. Stopped a step short, it is heard as one more cause, and the viewer asks what it has to do with them.

## How it is known

An anchor reports. The form of each sentence says how the newsroom knows what it says. A plain statement of what someone else does, demands or expects is heard as the newsroom vouching for it. [facts.md](/docs/writing/facts.md) "A hedge" gives the rule for any text; in a spoken report it sits in the ending or the attribution of every sentence. This is attribution and not softening: a count is stated flat, as its counter's count, and the "reportedly" with no one behind it that facts.md rules out stays out. Endings change with what the sentences carry; one ending on every sentence is a signal of machine-written text.

| The sentence carries | Its form | In Korean, for example |
|---|---|---|
| Something going on now | The progressive, or what is seen | -고 있습니다, -는 모습입니다 |
| A side's demand, position or account | That side as the subject, ongoing or as its position | -라는 입장입니다, -라고 설명하고 있습니다, -라고 주장하고 있습니다 |
| A count or an outcome | With who counted, and up to when | -로 집계됐습니다, -ㄴ 것으로 나타났습니다 |
| What a person said | With who and in what capacity; what another outlet reported or analysed, as heard through it, the outlet named on screen | -라고 말했습니다, -ㄴ 것으로 전해졌습니다, -라는 분석이 나왔습니다 |
| A plan or an announcement | As announced, with its condition | -ㄹ 예정입니다, -기로 했습니다 |
| What may follow | As an outlook, naming what decides it | -ㄹ 전망입니다, -ㄹ 수 있습니다 |
| A definition, or a fact of the record | Stated | -입니다 |

A language without such endings carries the same in attribution and tense ("has said", "is expected to", "by the ministry's count").

## For the ear

- **One number to a sentence**, with the thing it counts. Two leave the viewer unsure which was which and whether one is part of the other. Say a figure rounded, as people say it, and put the exact figure on the screen at the same moment.
- **Two of a kind.** Of parallel things (causes, demands, measures) the ear keeps two. Tell the two the sources weigh most, a sentence or two each, and say first that there are two where the sources group them so. A third is passed in a clause or left out. More hold only as a chain the sources state, each step the cause of the next: a chain is one line, and a list is several.
- **A subject sits close to its verb.** A long clause before the noun it describes is heard as words with no owner: split the sentence, the source first.
- **A name comes with what it is** at its first mention ("the agency that issues the permits"), and by its short form after that. A short form the viewer was never given is a new unknown.
- **Dates are said as the viewer lives them**, counted from the air date: yesterday, next Monday, the day before the vote. The absolute date follows the word in parentheses and is not read, so that the script can be dated again for another day. This is where the script leaves the writing set, which writes dates absolute.
- **A quote is spoken as a quote only when it is short and stands alone.** A longer one is said in plain words with its speaker, because the ear loses where a long quote ends, and the words as printed go on the screen as the quote. Words cut from the middle of a sentence keep what they point at, or are said plainly: half a quote that refers to something unsaid is a new question.
- **A person another outlet interviewed is that outlet's reporting.** The screen names the outlet with the quote.

## The close

The close is where the viewer hears the report end. It comes back to what the lead opened on and says where that goes from here: what decides it and when, as an outlook a source gives, said as one. Where no source looks ahead, it says where things stand at their widest (the week, the year, the count so far). It is a whole sentence about the story's own subject. A date or another body's step enters it only as the reason for the outlook, where a source says how it bears on the story; alone at the end, such a fact is heard as the start of something, and the report stops without ending. It does not list what the viewer just heard, and does not judge it, which would be the writer's own reading.

## Length

A report's length is its spoken length. Write the spoken form (every number, unit and symbol as it is said, the notes in parentheses dropped), count it in the unit the language is timed in (syllables for Korean and Japanese, words for English), and divide by the pace. The pace is the language's own for news reading with its pauses in it, so none are added: for Korean, 350 syllables a minute (illustrative); for another language, a figure from a source at hand, named in the checks. A reference the user gave is counted and used in its place. Keep within a twentieth of the target, since a reader's pace varies by as much. A script that runs long loses a sentence or a part; it is never packed tighter.
