# Competitors: who sells it, the company behind each, and when it came in

How the competition branch finds who already sells the item, names the company behind each service, dates its entry, reads its prices as bands and its revenue from filings, and how the review's competition section is laid out. Read by the competition branch and before the section is written.

## Who counts

A competitor sells the same result to the same payer. Two lists are kept apart from the start: those that sell to consumers and those that sell to businesses or institutions, since their prices, their sales and their numbers do not compare. What the buyers do today in place of any product (by hand, with a general tool, through a person they pay) is written as a third, short list of substitutes: it is what the item must beat first.

## Finding them

1. Search in the market's language, with the words a buyer uses for the job and not the product's category name; then with the names the first results compare themselves to.
2. Search the market's app stores with the same words, where the item could be an app.
3. Read a comparison article or a ranking as a pointer to names. Every name is then opened on its own site.
4. Stop adding names when two searches in a row bring none that is new, or when the budget's discovery share is spent, and say which in "Not seen".

## The company behind a service

The operator is read from the service's own pages, in this order, and the page that names it is the evidence:

1. The terms of service, whose opening clause names the company that provides the service.
2. The business details a seller's site carries, usually in the footer: on a Korean site the company's name, its registration number, its representative and its address (checked 2026-11).
3. The seller named on the app's store page, and the controller named in the privacy policy.

Where the pages disagree (a brand, a subsidiary and a parent), write each with its page. Where none names a company, the row says "operator unconfirmed", and the service stays in the table.

## When it came in

No page states an entry date, so it is bounded by what can be dated, and the row says which of these it rests on:

- The domain's registration date, from a registry lookup (RDAP or WHOIS).
- The effective date written in the terms of service, and that of their first version where the page keeps a history.
- The app's first release date on its store page.
- The start date of the oldest ad of its Page still running in the Meta Ad Library.
- The first press mention.

The earliest of them is "no later than". A brand older than the product (a company that added the service to an existing site) is dated by the terms and the ads, not by the domain.

## Revenue

- A Korean operator: `dart-company-figures`, run from the session and not from the branch. Its row gives the figures in won, the basis, the check and the proof that the site and the filing are one company. A row whose check is `matches` or `sums-up` enters the table as it is; `announced-only` enters marked as announced.
- A listed operator elsewhere: its own annual filing, read at the regulator's site, graded `filing`.
- No filing: what the company announced, graded `announced`, with the announcement's address. An adjective ("profitable", "record") is not a figure and is left out.
- An operator that sells many things: the segment where the filing has one; else the company's figure with a note that the service is a part of it.

## Prices

A price is read on the service's own price page, with the day it was read, as a band for each kind of product: the free tier and what it stops at, the one-off purchase, the subscription a month and a year, the price for a business where it is published. The band runs from the cheapest to the dearest of that kind across the competitors. One value does not describe a market, and a ratio of two single values (ours against theirs) is never written.

## Ads

Where a competitor sells through ads, `meta-ad-library-creative-research` reads its Page, run from the session at that skill's own stops. The competition section takes three things from its report: how many ads are active, the start date of the oldest one still running, and the hooks in a line.

## The table

One table for consumers and one for businesses, the same columns:

| Column | Holds |
|---|---|
| Name | The service, with its address |
| Operator | The company, or "operator unconfirmed", with the page that names it |
| Form | What is sold and how: an app, a one-off file, a subscription, a service a person performs |
| Price | The band and the plans, with the day read |
| Came in | "No later than" a date, and which evidence |
| Revenue | The figure, its basis and its check, or blank |
| Source | The addresses the row rests on, each with its grade |

A table of more than a dozen rows is split by the form of what is sold, each part under its own heading.

## The closest one, in depth

The competitor nearest the item gets a subsection: its funnel from the free step to each paid step with the price at each, what it covers and what it leaves out as its own pages show it, and its weak points as observations with their evidence (a review quoted with its address, a feature absent from its pages, a complaint repeated across reviews). The review's "where to win" is written from these observations; a judgment on the company is not.
