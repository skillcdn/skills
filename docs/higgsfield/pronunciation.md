# Pronunciation: what the video models do with sounds

Video models with native dialogue have no pronunciation dictionary, and a transcript often cannot show the fault. This file is the knowledge, per language, gathered from real runs of every Higgsfield skill of the repository; the procedure that uses it (how lines are written, how a take is read, how a retry is made) is each skill's own. A fault kept out of the first take costs nothing; one found in a take costs a retry; one found by the user costs their trust.

This is the one copy. A new outcome goes here with its date, the model and what was tried, and the sound moves to the list it belongs to; an agent running a skill without the repository at hand reports it in its delivery instead. Add a language section when a run teaches one.

## In any language

- **Spell for the ear in the prompt, from the first take.** A model reads letters. A word that is not said the way it is written goes into the take prompt the way it sounds, in the language's own script, while the intended line and the subtitle keep the spelling. This is the dictionary trick of text-to-speech tools; it has fixed every fault of the kinds listed below as fixed by spelling.
- **The first word of a take is said fastest.** Never a name there; give it a lead-in word, and ask for about half a second of silence before the first word.
- **An ordinary word that failed is said another way in the retry**, with a plainer word of the same meaning; the intended line and the subtitle change with it. A name or a key word is respelled, then its syllables are named after the line, then it goes on screen as text; it is never changed without the user.
- **Names and loanwords in another script** are written in the prompt the way the dialogue language says them (a Latin name in Hangul for Korean); the subtitle keeps the original spelling.
- **Only names and key words are kept clear of the sounds a model slurs.** Most words with those sounds come out right; a script rewritten around sounds loses its voice. Ordinary words are left as written and the review catches the rest.

## Korean

All of it is Seedance 2.5 speaking Korean at five to seven syllables a second, from the dated notes below; a new outcome moves a sound from one list to another.

- **Fixed by spelling for the ear**, so spelled that way from the first take: an aspirated cluster ("적혔다" as "저켰다", "축하해" as "추카해", "약혼자와" as "야콘자와"); a final consonant carried onto a particle ("…님의" as "…니메", "크레딧이" as "크레디시", "갚아" as "가파", "쪽은" as "쪼근"); a double final ("까닭" as "까닥"); an assimilated or tensed cluster ("식만" as "싱만", "첫날밤부터" as "천날빰부터"). Written this way from the first take, none of these words failed.
- **Fixed by naming the syllables in Latin letters after the line**, in a key word: ㅟ heard as ㅚ by the larger decodes ("취소" as "최소"; "chwi and so, a clear wi"), ㅡ heard as ㅜ ("증인" as "중인", "중위"; "jeung-in, an unrounded eu"), a plain ㅈ ("소재" as "소회"; "so and jae, with a clear j sound"). The line itself stays unchanged.
- **Slurred, and no spelling helped**: 져 and 겨 inside a word (heard as 서 and 려: "뒤져서" as "뒤서서"), "견적" (heard as "견약"), the counter "권" after a number (heard as "간", "건"). An ordinary word with these sounds that failed is said another way in the retry.
- **Smeared, replaced before the first take**: a noun ending in a consonant cluster before a vowel-initial particle ("진흙이나", "진흙에"), smeared into other syllables in several takes; use a plain synonym ("진창이나") or a consonant-initial continuation ("진흙 속에"); clusters before a consonant ("진흙발") are fine. A chain of Sino-Korean syllables or a coined term, unrecognized by both decodes; use a plain phrase of the same meaning.
- **A syllable added**: the subject particle "이" after a one-syllable noun ("돌이") came back doubled ("돌이가"); use another particle ("돌은") and say "no added syllables" in the prompt.
- **A name's vowel bent**: a two-syllable name with ㅓ was said with ㅗ even with a Latin respelling in the prompt; a name is what the viewer learns first, so leave it out of the line when the sentence survives, and never put it first.
- **Written differently, heard the same**, so no fault: ㅐ and ㅔ ("세로", "새로"), a loanword's vowel or final ("숏폼", "쇼폼"), a numeral for its word, spacing.
- **One decoder's ear, settled by the others**: a verb ending ("피운" written "피우는" by one decode), a plain initial written tense by one decode ("서 줘" as "써 줘") while two plain decodes agreed on the intended word; a whispered line that the medium model did not decode at all while the small and the largest did; a forced laugh right before a line that dropped every decode's probabilities under 0.1 while the envelope showed the speech and the words were right.

Respelling it in the prompt as "저켰다" while the intended line stayed "적혔다" fixed it on the first retry: Whisper then wrote "적혔다" at 0.98, and the syllable's onset carried about 50 milliseconds of aspiration noise against 10 to 20 in the failed takes.
- 2026-09-23, Seedance 2.5: a voice-over take started mid-word at frame 0 and lost its first consonant (묻는 was heard as 웃는); the retry, prompted for about half a second of silence before the line, came with a 0.47-second lead and the exact words.
- 2026-09-30, Seedance 2.5: a noun ending in ㅁ lost that final consonant before a particle in three places of one take (Whisper small and medium both wrote it without the ㅁ). Respelling it in the prompt the way it links to the particle ("…니메" for "…님의", "…니믄" for "…님은"), with the intended line unchanged, fixed it on the first retry (0.99); the retry then blurred another word, which the first take had clean, so the edit took that sentence from the first take.

The first word, a persona's name, was the only doubtful one: sung too fast for its vowel to be certain, on both takes, and a retry that asked for the name to be said clearly changed nothing and blurred another word.
