# The reader, the language and the register

Who a text is written for decides its language, its terms, its register and how much it says. This page holds what is settled before the first sentence: the reader, the language, the register and the voice, and plain words.

## Who reads it, and what they do with it

- Name the reader before writing: who they are, what they know already, and what they will do with the text. Where a skill has a question for it, the question is asked; otherwise the reader is derived from the request and stated at the first checkpoint.
- Write for what the reader does with the text. The reader of minutes is the person on the other side who has to act on them the day after the meeting, so every sentence there is something they can act on or check.
- A text that is spoken over and a text that is read alone are different texts. A deck presented in a room carries one message a slide; a deck read as a document before or after the meeting needs a way to find a section, its figures with their sources, and its facts in tables.
- A reader who knows nothing of the subject takes the story from the case that is theirs: the thing they own or use, a place, a sum or a business like their own. Look for those facts before general figures.
- Where the readers were in the room, cut what they already know: the project's name, a boundary nobody will contest.

## The language

- Write in the reader's language, the headings and the labels included. It is asked, or taken from the request. For a document sent to another party, it is the language of the material the user brought, unless the organization's records say otherwise; a report written for the user is in the user's language, whatever language its material is in.
- What an agent reads (a working agreement, the keys and fixed values of a data file) is in English; free text in the same file that a person reads is in that person's language.
- A text in a language is written in that language, not translated word by word: its honorifics and its tone, a product's name spelled as that language spells it.
- Use the terms the organization's earlier documents use. With none, take the plainest words of that language's business writing for the same things.
- One word for one thing across a text; add a word only when none fits.

## The register and the voice

- The register is taken from what the reader already reads: the organization's earlier documents of the same kind, or the reference whose format the text is built in, whose patterns are applied sentence by sentence.
- With nothing to take it from, the medium's own default holds where its skill gives one (the polite form of an app's interface or of a post, for example); with none, sentences are plain declaratives in the neutral written register (in Korean, 한다체), never the clipped note form.
- The stance and the tone are the user's to give. A stance (a report, a commentary, advocacy for a side) decides which facts and quotes come first and where the other side's reply sits; a tone decides how far a headline goes. Neither moves the floor of the facts ([facts.md](facts.md)).
- The voice is that of whoever speaks (the organization, the account, the figure), never the agent's. A first-person line stands only where someone speaks as themselves.

## Plain words

- Every word is one the reader knew before. A coined word, a brand's slogan word, or a word that reads as foreign in the reader's language is replaced by the ordinary term; a product's name stays a name. The reader's ear decides.
- The thing, not an abstraction standing in for it: what it does, in the words the subject's own page uses.
- No jargon beyond a name the reader needs, and nothing the text's tone rules out; a product's own tone may rule out exclamation marks and false urgency.
