# Story design

The reference is read as a story before it is read as shots, and the ad is written as a story before it is cut into shots. This page is the method for both, in any register: a legend told at night, a confession, a joke, a testimonial, a demo with a twist. A reference without lines (a montage that only shows the product) skips the story parts and keeps the measurements.

Three things went wrong in a run that read the reference well and still made a weaker ad, and each has a test below: the hook gave the ending away, the story ran on a stock trope instead of a rule of the product's own world, and the script was written to a mood ("slow, weighty") while the reference spoke a third faster.

## Reading the reference

Fill the brief's "Story engine", "Information order" and "Delivery" sections from the transcript and the frames.

| Question | What to write | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Form | On-camera dialogue, a narrator over pictures, a testimonial to camera, a demo, a text-only story. | The form decides how takes are made: one voice take under cut pictures, or one speaking take per shot. |
| Genre and register | The kind of story and the conventions it borrows (a folk tale, a deadpan joke, a courtroom confession); its tone words; the words it never uses. | The new story keeps the register and its conventions, not only its mood. |
| Rule of the world | The belief or principle the story runs on, in one sentence that begins "In this world, ...". | The ending is inevitable in hindsight only because of the rule. A story without one is a trope. |
| Device | The small, concrete, specific thing that carries the rule (an object, a number, a habit, a gesture), where it appears, whether it returns at the end. | The device is what the viewer remembers. An abstraction is not. |
| Figure | Who holds authority in the world (an elder, an expert, the product's own persona), and how the story proves it. | The product inherits the figure's authority at the end card. |
| Signature line | The one line a viewer could quote: the figure's own voice stating the rule. | The emotional center. Most references have exactly one. |
| Reveal | The last fact, and the sentence the viewer thinks right after it. | A reveal that takes two steps of inference is not felt. |

The information order is a table with one row per line: what the line adds, what it withholds, and its mark (hook, rule, exception, question, answer, reveal, call to action). The order is the mechanism; the surface is not.

## Writing the new story

The concept is written from the product's world on the reference's mechanism, in this order.

1. **Find the product's rule.** From the product brief: what the product claims to know, do or see, in its own vocabulary (its metaphors, its named features, its persona). Write the new rule as one sentence "In this world, ...", in the product's terms. Test: take the product's domain away; if the story still works, it is not this product's story, and another rule is needed.
2. **Choose the device.** One concrete, small thing from the product's own vocabulary or world that carries the rule and can return at the end. Prefer something the product's site itself names, but choose it for the viewer: a thing they already value or fear (money, a date, a door, a meal) beats a thing only the product knows (a page number, a mark, a setting). Never the reference's device.
3. **Cast the figure.** The product's own persona when the site presents one, else a new figure with authority in the new world. The figure's acts are fiction and read as fiction; claims about the product stay the site's. A new figure who speaks to the camera as someone who uses the product is not read as fiction: they say what the product's page says it does and report no result of their own (money made, time saved, a problem gone).
4. **Write five hooks and keep one.** The five use at least three different stakes (money, death, the body, a child, a debt, a taboo, a vow). Score the reference's own hook against the table first, so that the bar is the reference's; then score each candidate, and drop any that fails a row or scores under the reference on one. The runner-up is shown at the plan checkpoint.

| The hook must | Test |
|---|---|
| Need no decoding | Every word is one the viewer knew before the ad: no brand term, no coined word, no name that carries nothing yet. The figure enters as what they are, a title that says their power in a word (the midwife who has never lost a child, the tailor who cuts without measuring), never as a name; the name belongs on the end card. |
| Carry a stake the viewer already has | The odd act touches something everyone values or fears: money, death, the body, food, a child, a debt, a vow, a taboo. A detail of the product's craft (a dot, a page, a dial, a setting) carries no stake until the story gives it one, so it cannot open the ad. |
| Break a norm | The act is odd because the viewer knows how it should go: a lender who never forgives a day's delay waits a year for one debtor. The norm and the breach are felt in the same breath, which is also what makes the figure formidable before anything is explained. |
| Be concrete | It names a thing, a person, a number or an act, not a feeling, a theme or an idea. |
| Be specific | One person, one exception, one number ("only that debtor", "always one day more"). |
| Not add up | It states something the viewer cannot resolve from what they know. |
| Withhold the turn | It names the anomaly, never its meaning. The reveal is not in the hook, and no later line repeats the hook. A hook that is the ending in disguise ("the day the ledger closed") fails here. |
| Fit the window | At the reference's measured rate it fits the reference's hook window. |
| Lead somewhere | The next line can begin the rule or the exception without a detour. |

The last gate is the thumb test: read the kept hook as a stranger scrolling past, who has never heard of the product and will not read a caption. If it stops them only once they know what a word means, or who the figure is, it fails, whatever the table says. Two kinds of hook passed every row and failed this test: one that named the day the figure's own tool stopped (the ending in disguise), and one that asked why the figure left a small mark of the craft in one customer's copy only (three words to decode, nothing at stake).

5. **Write the signature line.** In the figure's voice, stating the rule the way a person says it (a proverb, a rebuke, a joke), short enough to quote, in the register. It answers the question someone inside the story asks.
6. **Write the reveal.** One concrete fact, stated flat, that re-reads the device and the figure's act. Write beside it the sentence the viewer thinks next; it must take one step. Nothing is explained after the reveal. The end card carries the call to action, unless the reference speaks one (step 7).
7. **Order the lines** in a table like the reference's, with the same marks. Each line adds one fact. No line says what the picture already shows. The rule is planted before the exception. The reveal is the last line of the story. A reference that speaks on after its turn (a direct-response ad does: the product in a line or two, the offer, a call to action) keeps those marks after the reveal in its order, in the product's own words and with only an offer its page states; they add facts and never explain the reveal. Every line is heard, not read: a homophone that changes the meaning is replaced ([regeneration.md](regeneration.md) "Pronunciation").
8. **Match the density** (below). Then cut the story into shots (SKILL.md phase 3).

## Density and tempo

Pace is measured, never guessed. A calm reference is often fast, and a script written to a mood drags.

- **Measure the reference** from the word-timed transcript: the speaking time (the sum of the speech segments), the unit count (Hangul, kana and CJK: one syllable per character; other scripts: words), the rate (units per second of speech), the lines per ten seconds of runtime, the longest pause between lines, the speech share of the runtime, and the hook window (the end of the first line). The brief's "Delivery" section carries them; the sandbox pass prints them.
- **Write to the rate.** Target units = the reference's rate times the new ad's speaking time (the runtime minus the end card, times the reference's speech share). The script stays within ten percent of the target, and the line count within one line per ten seconds of the reference's. Both numbers are stated at the plan checkpoint.
- **Direct the delivery from the numbers.** The take prompt says the pace in words that match the measurement: brisk and continuous, pauses under half a second, one longer pause only where the reference has one. "Slow", "unhurried" or "weighty" appear only when the measurement says so.
- **Size a voice take to the speech.** A voice-over take lasts the expected speaking time plus about a second, never the length of the ad: a longer take is padded with pauses and dead air.
- **Review the take against the numbers** (SKILL.md phase 7): its rate and its longest pause. Pauses are tightened in code first. A take still more than twenty percent under the target rate, or with a dead pause where the reference has none, is "dragging" and is regenerated with the pace stated in numbers ([regeneration.md](regeneration.md)).

## What the user sees

At the plan checkpoint, before the shots, each in one line of plain words: the hook and the runner-up; the rule of the world; the device; the signature line; the reveal with the sentence the viewer will think; then every line as it will be spoken, in order, with who says it; and the script's density against the reference. The lines are the ad the user is about to pay for: a summary of them, or a pointer to a file, does not stand in for them. The user need not know these terms, and any of it can be changed there.
